NanoTab vs Payman: Which Should You Use for AI Agent Payments?

2 November 2026 · 6 min read

Should you use NanoTab or Payman for AI agent payments? If you're an indie developer who wants per-call USDC micropayments live today with near-zero fees, use NanoTab. If you're a team that wants fiat-friendly agent payments with existing banking infrastructure, compliance, and human payouts, use Payman.

Both live in the "money for AI agents" space, but they solve different halves of the problem. Payman (founded 2024, reportedly raised ~$13.8M with backing from Visa and Coinbase Ventures, per secondary sources) built an AI-to-human payment platform: agents get managed wallets, spend policies, and can pay humans or services in fiat and crypto. NanoTab (open-source, 2026) is a lightweight x402 paywall: wrap any HTTP endpoint so agents pay USDC per call on Base, and you keep 99.9%. Here's the honest breakdown.

Fees: published vs undisclosed

NanoTab's fees are public and tiny: 0.1% self-hosted, 2% through the managed gateway. On a $0.05 API call that's a fraction of a cent self-hosted. There is no subscription, no seat, no minimum.

Payman does not publish a pricing page. Its terms of use state a fee is collected on each transaction, and secondary sources describe a percentage-plus-flat structure per live transaction — but no exact number is publicly confirmed, so treat any precise figure you see quoted online with suspicion. What is clear is the model: you're paying for managed wallets, compliance, fiat rails, and policy enforcement, not just settlement. That's a platform fee, not a micropayment fee — appropriate for payouts to humans, expensive for $0.02 API calls.

Setup complexity: minutes vs hours

NanoTab's pitch is one prompt: point it at your HTTP endpoint and it generates the x402 paywall middleware. No account, no signup, no KYB. Your agent gets a wallet, the buyer pays USDC on Base, funds land in your wallet. There is genuinely nothing to sign up for.

Payman is a proper platform integration: create a developer account, get OAuth client credentials, start with a TSD test wallet (1,000 test units, free), then generate live credentials and add USD or USDC wallets when you're ready for real money. Live wallets require KYC with government ID. Sandbox-to-live is reportedly smooth — same code, swapped credentials — but it's hours of account and identity work before your first real payment, not minutes. That's the right tradeoff for moving real dollars to real humans.

Fiat vs crypto rails: Payman's clear strength

This is where the two diverge most. Payman is multi-rail: USD wallets (hosted via Stripe, ACH for US developers) alongside USDC on Solana and Base. An agent can pay a contractor in dollars, settle an invoice over ACH, or send USDC internationally — all under human-set policies like spending limits, approval thresholds, and pre-approved payee lists.

NanoTab is USDC-on-Base only, built on the x402 HTTP-402 protocol. That's a deliberate constraint: one chain, one asset, zero gas for the seller, sub-second settlement. If your buyers hold USDC on Base (increasingly the default for agent wallets), it's frictionless. If they need to pay a human in dollars, NanoTab simply can't do that — Payman is the answer.

Credit where due: Payman's compliance posture (SOC 2, PCI, KYC/BSA/AML readiness, isolated wallets per agent) is exactly what banks and regulated teams need. They're reportedly expanding into agentic banking for credit unions and community banks. NanoTab has an opt-in risk gate (SafeSpend, powered by Jev) that scores transactions, but it's log-only advisory, not enforcement — it won't replace compliance tooling.

Use-case direction: mirror images

The key insight is that these products face opposite directions:

Payman = agents paying out. The canonical flow is an AI agent hiring a human (freelance task, data labeling, expert review), paying a vendor, or moving money between accounts. The agent holds a managed wallet; the human sets the guardrails; money flows agent → human or agent → service, often in fiat.

NanoTab = APIs charging per call. The canonical flow is a developer wrapping an endpoint so that any agent (or human) pays a few cents of USDC per request. Money flows buyer → seller, machine-to-machine, at micropayment scale.

These are complementary, not competitive. A team could plausibly use Payman to pay human annotators in USD while using NanoTab to monetize the API the resulting model serves. If your mental model is "which one wins agent payments," you're asking the wrong question — they serve different sides of the transaction.

Verdict: it depends on which direction money flows

Use NanoTab if: you're an indie dev, researcher, or small team; you want to charge per API call in USDC live today; you value open-source and self-hosting; fees under 1% matter to you.

Use Payman if: your agents need to pay humans or services, especially in fiat; you need KYC, spend policies, and audit-ready infrastructure; you want USD + USDC rails with a funded vendor behind them.

Use both if: money flows both ways in your system — Payman for agent-to-human payouts, NanoTab for per-call API monetization. They're genuinely complementary.

The agentic-payments space is early enough that "which platform" matters less than "are your agents transacting at all." Pick the one that matches your money flow, ship it, iterate. (As of November 2026.)
